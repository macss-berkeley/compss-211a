Baselines and confusion tables

Week 10

Two methods predict the verdict of twenty short replies. This page scores them against a baseline, counts their mistakes in a confusion table, and compares them fairly.

The twenty replies were written for this page in the style of replies on r/AmItheAsshole. Each reply gave a verdict: NTA (the poster was not in the wrong) or YTA (the poster was in the wrong). The verdict word has been removed, so a method has to work it out from the rest of the reply.

The classifier is TF-IDF with logistic regression, trained on 1,035 real AITA replies with the verdict word removed. Gemini is gemini-3.5-flash-lite, given the instruction below. Its labels are from one run.

Gemini's system instruction 
You read replies to posts on the Reddit forum r/AmItheAsshole, where people describe a
conflict and ask whether they were in the wrong. The verdict at the start of each reply
has been removed.

Predict the verdict the reply gave:
- NTA: the reply says the poster was not in the wrong.
- YTA: the reply says the poster was in the wrong.

Also give a reason of one short sentence.

1.Start with a baseline

The simplest baseline ignores the text and always guesses the same verdict. Its accuracy is the share of replies with that verdict. Choose a guess.

    (replies["verdict"] == "NTA").mean()   # 0.6
    A method scores 0.60 on these replies. What does that show?

    2.Count the mistakes in a confusion table

    A confusion table counts each pair of true verdict (rows) and predicted verdict (columns). Correct predictions are on the diagonal. Choose a method, then click a cell to see its replies. 

      pd.crosstab(replies["verdict"], replies["classifier"])
      How many YTA replies did the classifier call NTA?

      3.Accuracy for each verdict

      One accuracy number can hide a method that never predicts a verdict. Divide each row of the confusion table by its total to get the accuracy for each verdict. 

      replies["correct"] = replies["classifier"] == replies["verdict"]
replies.groupby("verdict")["correct"].mean()
      You want to find the YTA replies. Which is more useful: always guessing NTA or the classifier?

      4.Compare on the same texts

      Gemini got every reply on this page right. These replies were written to be clear. The same instruction was also run on 20 real AITA replies with the verdict word removed, 10 NTA and 10 YTA.

      Real replies: one Gemini run. Gemini can give a different label to the same reply on another run.

      A report says: “Gemini scored 1.00 and the classifier 0.35.” What is wrong with it?
      Why does always guessing NTA score 0.50 on the real replies but 0.60 here?

      Summary

      • Always guessing the most common label is the baseline. A method has to beat it to show that it learned something from the text.
      • The baseline depends on the texts: 0.60 on these replies, 0.50 on a sample with as many YTA as NTA replies.
      • A confusion table shows which labels a method mixes up. Correct predictions are on the diagonal.
      • Report accuracy for each label as well as the total, so a method that never predicts a label cannot hide.
      • Compare methods on the same texts. Texts written to be clear can make any method look better than it is on real data.
      • Read the texts a method got wrong before you trust its labels.
